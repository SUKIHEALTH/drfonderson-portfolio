Healthcare & Leadership 2025

What will my doctor be like in 50 years?

Dr. Sabine Fonderson

Physician · Health AI Innovator · Keynote Speaker

Created on 2019-03-28 14:17

Published on 2019-03-28 14:33

I have been a medical doctor for over 10 years and now that I am doing my general practice training it all seems to be falling into place. My roles have taken on various forms, from the conventional prescriber to taking on a quasi- secretarial function while I arranged patient transportation from the hospital to a care home. I have also taken a social-carer role when I visited the home of a single mother struggling with personal and health issues. I think I have a good idea of what it takes to be a doctor of my community, a generalist. Now the question that I ask myself is; ‘what will my general practitioner look like 50 years from now?’

I search for this answer and dig into the history of what general practice actually is. A fact that is new to me is that before the 20th century, general practitioners worked as private traders (or modern day entrepreneurs) and only treated people who could afford to pay for the services (Kmietowicz, BMJ 2006 ). In the  early 1900’s the National Insurance Act was introduced in the United Kingdom (UK) and made it compulsory for those on low income to have healthcare. That is when local insurance companies started to contract general practitioners to provide services through a capitation fee.

Just before 1950 when everyone in the UK was allowed free primary care, the demand overwhelmed general practitioners. Since then, not much has changed. In fact, the future looks quite dismal in many regions. Not only in the United Kingdom, but general practice as a whole is struggling in other parts of Europe as well.

The major contributing factors that cause this crisis of identity are:  

  1. Increase in healthcare costs.
  2. Increase ageing population. General practitioners will be expected to take more time to deal with multi etiological diseases.
  3. Increase chronic, complex disease. The emphasis will lie on shifting from diagnostic care to prevention care in order to limit the burden of complex, chronic disease
  4. More people living at longer home longer. This will effectively mean that general practitioners will perform more home visits in the future.

The fifth factor that we should take very seriously is the rise in shortage of healthcare professionals. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), in 2035 the world will have a shortage of nearly 13 million healthcare providers. The vast majority of this deficit will be in regions like Asia and Sub-Saharan Africa. In the Netherlands, an estimated 800,000 trained doctors and nurses will be needed by 2040.

What needs to change?

Healthcare care is provided by primary, secondary and tertiary sectors. There is a pyramid-like structure where a patient rises to the top layer depending on the severity of disease. General practitioners are primary care providers, their role is to maintain health, and when needed make referrals to specialists. This is how it has worked for a very long time, however the professional barriers that have been created between each sector have to some extent made access to healthcare challenging and disrupted the role of general practitioners. Think for instance in countries were seeing a specialist is just one phone call away. Compared to countries like the The Netherlands, one can see specialist only after a referral letter has been produced by the General practitioner. This can cause delays and frustration among patients who are used to a different type of access to specialist, diagnostics and so on. With this in mind, proposals are in place to shift the way healthcare is structured. A vision to a more linear form of access, were all sectors are lined together on one layer and care is merged through a network of professionals. Eliminating barriers and promoting inter-professional collaboration.  

What will this mean for the future of general practice?

Care should be much closer to home. Numerous initiatives have been implemented to encourage seniors to stay at home longer, supporting them in daily activities and closely monitor their conditions remotely. Identifying teams that can manage the program would include a care manager, a general practitioner, specialist and social care provider.

Care needs to be cheaper. Besides the rising costs of healthcare services, drugs are the second largest healthcare expense. The largest portion of this if for prescription medication. By reducing the prescribing habits of general practitioners and educating patients on lifestyle interventions based on their own individual needs and relevance, this can lower costs. A sign of things to change is the fact that in a few years the patent on many prescription drugs will run out which will lower the costs of making medication significantly.

Care needs to based on personal responsibility. The focus needs to be more on what the patient wants. Can patients define what a good life means to them. If care is driven towards taking ownership of one’s health, then self-management can lead to individualized objectives which have a higher rate of success. General practitioners will become more mindful to environmental, genetic, physical and emotional factors when giving advice and treatment.

Care needs to involve technology. General practices are taking advantage of technological developments to provide care to their patients. This is taking place in the form

  • Informative websites
  • E-consultations
  • Online prescription of repeat medication
  • Online referral systems and lab requests
  • E-health tools for education and care management

Given the paradigm shift healthcare is facing, where control and responsibilities are moving more towards the consumers or patients, I hope that 50 years from now my general practitioner will

  • See less patients, thereby increasing the time spent per patient
  • Reduce the amounts of specialist referrals and contributing to lowering costs of healthcare in general
  • Run his or her practice 80% remotely thereby creating a better life-work balance

More importantly, I hope my general practitioner will continue to encourage me to take responsibility for my own health.
