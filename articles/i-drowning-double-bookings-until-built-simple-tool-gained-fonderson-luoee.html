I Was Drowning in Double-Bookings Until I Built This Simple Automation Tool And Gained Back 5 Hours Of Time.

Created on 2025-06-15 16:02

Published on 2025-06-16 10:45

How many times have you said “Sure, let’s schedule something,” and then never followed up?

If you’re a doctor managing care in a second language, I’m guessing more than once. You’re not alone, I’ve been there too.

For years, appointment coordination was eating up hours of my week. Between vague email requests, overlapping calendars, and communication across five different platforms, I was spending over five hours weekly just trying to stay on top of it all.

And even with support, I was still dropping the ball.

Why This Happens, even with a VA

There’s a quiet kind of chaos that comes with working across systems. A patient asks to reschedule via email. A colleague messages you on LinkedIn. A vendor follows up on WhatsApp. You say yes, mentally slot it in, and then shift back to the clinical task in front of you.

By the time you remember the message, it’s either too late or as in my case more often than none it was double-booked.

My VA helped enormously. She created reminders on the platform I actually use, and followed up when people didn’t respond. But no matter how proactive she was, the process was still fragile. It relied on manual coordination, lots of back-and-forth, and ultimately me being available to approve or correct things.

And when you’re running between patients and other practice management related tasks, approvals don’t always happen.

Three Calendars. No Sync. No System.

At some point, I realized I was maintaining three separate calendars: one for clinic hours, one for business meetings, and one for my personal life.

They didn’t talk to each other.

This meant every appointment needed to be cross-checked manually, by me or my VA. Any time we missed a detail, I’d end up in two places at once. And with emergencies and overbooked clinic days, those scheduling gaps started to create real stress.

Eventually I hit a point where I thought: This cannot be the best we can do.

So I built an automation system.

What I Needed From It

Not bells and whistles. Just something that worked quietly in the background.

Here’s what I wanted:

  • Emails read and processed automatically

  • Appointment requests extracted, even vague ones like “let’s meet next week”

  • Calendar events created instantly without me doing it manually

  • Follow-up emails sent when information was missing

  • Flexibility to handle messages in multiple languages

What Went Wrong (And Why It Mattered)
I ran over 40 test emails. None of them created an event). I spent hours debugging, checking date formats (why are there so many?), adjusting time zones, even rebuilding the whole system from scratch.
Still nothing.
Eventually, I found the issue: a faulty “regex pattern” in the text parser. It wasn’t pulling the date and time correctly from the email body, so the rest of the automation never had the data it needed to move forward.
And here’s the hard part: It didn’t throw an error. It just failed silently.
So while I thought the system was running, it wasn’t doing anything at all.

What I’m Building Next: One-Click Rescheduling
Emergencies come up. When they do, I need to cancel or shift all non-clinical appointments, fast. My next goal is a one-click reschedule tool. Something that can instantly find a new time, notify the other party, and update my calendar without needing manual input.
Because when patient care needs my attention, I want to shift admin work in seconds, not hours.
If You’re a Healthcare VA or Practice Manager
This kind of automation doesn’t just serve the doctor. It can scale across multiple clinicians, languages, and systems. It can reduce the volume of messages you send. It can create consistency in how appointments are handled. And most importantly, it can protect your own time.
No more copy-pasting, double-checking, or waiting on replies for basic scheduling.
The Takeaway
In medicine, small errors can have big consequences. Automation is no different.
One broken pattern in a text parser cost me weeks of frustration. Fixing it gave me hours of time and a sense of control I hadn’t felt in a long while.
If you’re spending more than 15 minutes a day on scheduling, it’s worth asking: What would this look like if it were automated properly?
Start with the tool that’s draining your time.
For most of us, that’s the calendar.
