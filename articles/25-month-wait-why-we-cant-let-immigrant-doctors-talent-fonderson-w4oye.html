The 25-Month Wait

In March 2025, Medisch Contact revealed a crisis: more than 150 internationally trained doctors in the Netherlands are waiting up to 25 months to take the BI-toets, the final step before medical licensure.

The BI-toets (Beroepsinhoudelijke Toets) is a professional medical exam with three parts:

  • A general medical knowledge test

  • A clinical reasoning test

  • A practical exam with simulated patients (actors)

The practical exam is only offered a few times per year, at one location: Radboudumc in Nijmegen.

In 2024, the AKV (language/general skills test) was removed to help accelerate the process. But the system bottlenecked. More candidates could apply, yet the number of test slots remained the same. The main problem now is that many elligible health professional candidates are now waiting over two years to take the test.

Why It Matters

During these long waits, doctors are often:

  • Unable to work clinically

  • Overqualified for entry-level roles

  • Underqualified (legally) for medical roles

As a former president of the Vereniging Buitenlands Gediplomeerde Artsen (VBGA), I’ve spoken to many immigrant doctors who have navigating this system. I’ve seen the emotional, professional, and financial strain it causes.

And while the consequences may not be visible yet, they’re building.

We’re standing at the intersection of three threats:

  1. An aging population with complex care needs

  2. A shrinking clinical workforce

  3. Continued underfunding in healthcare

We can’t afford to waste talent. And we certainly can’t afford to let brilliant, motivated doctors fade into invisibility.

Doctors Aren’t Asking for Shortcuts, Just Opportunities

Internationally trained doctors want to meet high standards. They accept the exam process. But what happens while they wait?

We must offer ways for them to stay active, stay seen, and stay sharp.

This newsletter is for those doctors. And for every clinician navigating medicine in a second language. If the system doesn’t have space for you yet, let us build onne that works for you.

My Own Waiting Period

When I moved to the UK as a trained doctor, I was required to pass the IELTS, even though English is my first language, I achieved my medical diploma in the Netherlands. During the two-month wait, I refused to let my skills go to waste.

Here’s what I did:

  • Wrote patient education content for Hearst International

  • Edited protocols for health ministries in Cameroon and Dubai

  • Tutored international medical students and helped place them in internships in Africa

  • Built, sold digital systems and templates for clinical efficiency

That period taught me something vital: waiting doesn’t mean standing still.

4 Things You Can Do While You Wait

1. Start a Micro-Clinic (Ethically and Legally)

Services may include:

  • Health coaching

  • Lifestyle support

  • Chronic illness management

  • Patient education for immigrant populations

You’re not diagnosing or prescribing, but you are supporting, educating, and empowering. This is part of my Ideal Practice Launch program.

2. Write and Edit Medical Content

Use your medical knowledge to:

  • Write patient-friendly articles

  • Translate health letters and lab results

  • Create multilingual health education materials

This is how I earned income and built credibility during my own wait.

3. Contribute to Research

Many departments accept unregistered doctors to support:

  • Literature reviews

  • Protocol writing

  • Scientific editing

Yes, it requires persistence. I’ve helped dozens of doctors craft emails, tailor CVs, and prepare motivation letters to land these opportunities.

Once in, you’ll need to produce real output. That’s why I created the phdPLANR — a 12-week structured journal designed for internationally trained doctors who want to publish their first article.

4. Offer Cultural and Language Support

Use your multilingual skills to:

  • Help patients understand test results

  • Serve as a medical guide or consultant in clinics

  • Support aging patients and their families with navigation

You may not be licensed yet, but you are still a trusted interpreter of medical meaning. This builds trust, reputation, and credibility.
