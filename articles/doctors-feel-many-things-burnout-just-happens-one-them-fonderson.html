Doctors feel many things, burnout just happens to be one of them

Dr. Sabine Fonderson

Physician · Health AI Innovator · Keynote Speaker

Created on 2015-12-16 00:43

Published on 2015-12-16 00:52

If you have never experienced burnout, I envy you. Imagine you are in a river and suddenly you get your foot stuck under a tree branch and the tide is pulling you one way whilst the branch is pulling you another way.

You have your head above water, you can breathe, you are not drowning...yet. But you can’t move. You are being pushed on either side yet you are not moving.

This is how I felt a few years ago. There was no way for me to go, and worst part is, I think I was in denial because I just did not know what was going on. I was not depressed, at least not by the diagnostic criteria. I enjoyed eating food, I was not crying at the drop of a hat, I slept (although less hours due to my shift work).

What ever the feeling was, I knew it was starting to affect my work performance. But I did not want to face the consequences of having to go to work and admit to colleagues that I think I have burnout. So I put up with the work load and dealt with it.

I don’t know what changed all of a sudden, I no longer felt stuck. I could move again, but this time it was at a much slower pace.

Then one day I got a comment  from a consultant saying he felt I was not meeting the targets of seeing patients in the emergency department and he was worried what would happen when I was on night shifts.

I looked at him puzzled, of course I met targets, I have done many night shifts before, this is the first time I had heard such a remark. Had he noticed that I was working at a slower pace?

So when my night shift came the following week, I worked hard and met my ‘targets’. I am not sure how many that was, was it an arbitrary number or was it because none of my patients had breached? I am not sure. But that following week I felt stuck again, the same feeling as being in that river. 

Burnout has no clinical definition, it is not found in either the ICD-10 or in DSM-IV diagnostic criteria. 

How does a doctor get diagnosed with burnout then?

According to research a the Karolinska Institute in Sweden

“It (burnout) usually affects people who are very committed to work. One day they wake up and they just can’t get out of bed. Then they take a few weeks’ sick leave, but they don’t improve,”

Interestingly the team of researchers state that people can function well under high level of stress, but once their sleep gets disrupted this is the key factor to developing burnout.

To test this hypothesis, the researchers took EEG readings of 35 patients who had called in sick from work for more than 3 months. Each patient had signs of extreme sleep disruption. In fact, they were getting as little as 5 hours of sleep every night compared to healthy people.

This would should not be rocket science to the average man and woman. But for me as a doctor I thrived during medical school boasting how little sleep I got and I still passed my exam. Sleep deprivation has become such a part of my life that I did not think it would or should affect me that much. I know this is really very poor judgement on my part as a doctor who always tells my patients to get some more rest when I know they are overworked.

So what did I do to prevent burnout?

I started making more conscious decisions on the type of shifts I would take on. Unfortunately night shift is still very much part of emergency medicine job description. So I guess I need to get used to entering that river, this time I will come prepared, I will bring a mattress and get some sleep.
