A pill for every orifice

Created on 2017-07-20 15:52

Published on 2017-07-20 16:02

Drug labelling instructions for patients are complicated. It never ceases to amaze me how many patients describe their medication in sizes and colours. When I ask them what prescription drugs they are on, I get responses like,

‘Well, there’s the tiny wee red tablet I take twice before going to bed’ or

‘The big orange and green one which looks like an oval shape and can only be taken before meals’.

Drug labels impart a wealth of information: the therapeutic substance of the drug, its product name, the diseases which can be cured or relieved, a list of circumstances in which the drug might not be suitable, and why a doctor should be contacted if some strange reaction should occur. On a leaflet enclosed with the drug, patients are reminded when to take it, how often and how it should be taken.

If one is fascinated by this information there is more, on the type of colouring used, inactive ingredients and the exact temperature at which it should be stored. It’s enough to get you to sleep if you have insomnia.

One night, when I was about to finish my shift, a 44‐year‐old male patient was admitted in some pain. When he saw me, he repeated the same thing that a paramedic and two nurses had already heard, ‘Oh, I’m in so much pain.’

I did what I have been taught to do, and asked, ‘What have you taken for the pain while at home?’

‘My family doctor prescribed me some tablets, which my wife collected the other day. They were the really big white ones, you know? They tasted disgusting. Every time I took one, I gagged and threw it back up. I just couldn’t handle the pain anymore.’

‘No, I don’t know the big white pills you’re referring to. Do you have them with you so I can check?’ He reached in his coat pocket and handed me a box of tablets. ‘Well that explains everything,’ I said to him. ‘The reason they’re not having any effect is because these are suppositories.’

His diagnosis came back as possible kidney stones. I recall many patients with similar conditions, and their pain is so excruciating they compare it unfavourably with childbirth. A few days later we received a card and a box of chocolates from him and his wife, written with a touch of humour:

“Dear A&E staff, It is a ‘miracle’ I am now pain-free. Thank you for the care you gave. From now on I have taken the habit to read before I swallow my pills. I now know which pill goes in which hole.”
