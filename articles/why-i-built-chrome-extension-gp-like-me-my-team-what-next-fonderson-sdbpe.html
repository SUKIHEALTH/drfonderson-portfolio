Something happened last week that was completely different from my usual day of navigating clinical work in a second language.

I wasn’t writing a referral. I wasn’t diagnosing. I wasn’t documenting in Dutch.

I was building a tool to help me be more efficient.

And not just any tool, I built a Chrome extension using code (assisted by #OpenAI), that solved a problem I couldn’t ignore anymore. And then I built another. One was accepted by the Chrome Web Store. The other one is for my team, they had an issue, I came up with a practical solution.

As a doctor who practices in a second language, that moment meant something extra. Not just because of the tech, but because the tools I created didn’t need translation. They solved problems across any language: inefficiency, overload, and the quiet fear of making small but costly mistakes.

It Started With Frustration

My first Chrome extension was born out of something simple but deeply familiar: the exhaustion of repeating myself.

As GPs, we all have phrases we use, especially when trying to reassure patients with non-acute symptoms. It’s the rhythm of our work. After enough years, you don’t just explain; you guide. And you develop a way of saying things that makes sense, keeps patients safe, and helps you move forward.

I often rely on TextExpander for this. It works well until it didn't. Something changed. After an update to our EHR, it started glitching. I’d open the record, try to insert a snippet, and suddenly the whole system would freeze. Restart. Re-log. Re-do. Again and again.

And here’s the thing: when you work in your second language, you need those anchors. You don’t want to spend time thinking about word order. You want to focus on the patient, on the plan, on staying present.

So I built something that didn’t rely on someone else’s system. A browser-based snippet bank, quick, clean, in both English and Dutch. I click. I copy. I paste. Done.

It’s not fancy. But it’s mine. And it works so I will keep on building on it.

Because the truth is, we’re constantly adapting to broken systems. The moment you get comfortable, an update comes and breaks your flow. The tools that used to make things easier now slow you down. And that’s not just inefficient. It’s demoralizing.

This extension is my reminder that when everything around you breaks, your own knowledge and creativity are still your strongest assets.

I thought this was it, but because I built it and it works, I continued building and then something else happened.

Blood Pressure Logs: 84 Numbers and a Whole Lot of Resistance

While showing my snippet extension to my team, a much bigger pain point came up, something so common we’d stopped noticing how broken it was. The Blood Pressure logs

Patients bring in 7-day diaries. Two readings in the morning. Two in the evening. Multiply that by systolic, diastolic, and pulse and you’ve got 84 entries.

Now guess who’s averaging them?

My assistants are scared they’ll make mistakes. My nurse practitioners don’t want to do it at all. Both are right.

So I did what I’ve started doing more and more this year: I listened. I mapped it out. I built something.

A Second Extension: Built in Hours, Saving Hours

This new tool lives right in your browser. You open it like a calculator. You type the values in exactly as the patient wrote them. You click. You get averages.

No switching to Excel. No formatting. No mental gymnastics.

It’s built inside your workflow and not outside of it.

Why is this useful for me and my team?

Because managing hypertension isn’t just about target values. It’s about tracking accurate, up-to-date readings in a way that’s safe, fast, and usable. Especially when patients are self-monitoring at home.

This extension removes the hesitation and reduces the risk. That’s the kind of tool we need more of.

Why Chrome? Why Not Excel?

Because we don’t need another spreadsheet that opens in another window.

We need focused, browser-native tools. Tools that exist exactly where we work. Tools that feel natural, no switching tabs, no copy-paste errors, no guesswork.

This is what I mean by building for doctors in the real world, especially those of us practicing in systems, languages, and cultures we weren’t trained in.

How I Did It. A Simple Breakdown

It is not fancy, it is practical and my team no longer have to feel anxious of making mistakes or rushing their already long list of tasks.

What This Means for You

You don’t need to be a native speaker, or a senior developer, to build meaningful solutions.

You just need to listen to the people doing the work. Ask better questions. Start small. Let AI fill the gaps when you can’t.
