What Would You Do?Two Real Cases That Show Why Communication Can Make or Break a Doctor’s Career

Created on 2025-04-09 17:53

Published on 2025-04-10 11:15

If you're an immigrant doctor navigating a second language, you already know this:

Medicine is universal, but communication isn’t.

You can follow the guidelines, master the clinical reasoning, and still feel unsure in the moment. You can be clinically right and still misunderstood. And sometimes, that gap between intention and expression can end up in court.

This week, I’m doing something different. Below are two real cases, but before I share what happened, I want you to put yourself in the doctor’s shoes. Decide what you would do.

Case 1: The Pediatrician and the Hesitant Parents

You're a pediatric oncologist.

You meet the parents of a nine-year-old boy who recently had brain surgery for medulloblastoma. You explain that chemo and radiation are the next steps. Time matters and treatment ideally starts within a several days.

But the parents are hesitant. They say they need a few days to think and process everything.

This is your first and only consultation with them.

Do you:

A) Respect their concern and offer a follow-up in a few days

B) Immediately report them for medical child abuse due to delay in accepting care

What would you do?

Here’s what actually happened:

The doctor filed the report. The parents were shocked and distressed. The court later ruled in favor of the parents, stating that one difficult consultation did not justify a report to child abuse agency. The judge emphasized that more time and trust-building were needed before escalating the case.

Takeaway: In this type of situation where time to decide and act is very important, communication isn't just about medical urgency, it’s about relational safety. Trust doesn’t happen in 30 minutes. Especially not across cultural or linguistic barriers.

Case 2: The GP Who Missed the Meaning Behind the Words

You're a GP. Your patient is recovering from recent vascular surgery and has a history of COPD.

She comes in saying she feels weak, tired, and just “not herself.” No major findings. Her lungs are clear, oxygen is acceptable.

But her partner says, “She’s worse than she says. This is not like her.”

Do you:

A) Send her home with advice to rest and monitor

B) Refer her for urgent hospital review despite no obvious findings

Here’s what happened:

The GP chose option A. The patient had Legionella pneumonia. She was admitted to ICU days later and died. The court found that vague symptoms in a high-risk patient should have triggered more investigation. The partner’s concern was a missed opportunity for escalation.

Takeaway: Communication is more than listening to symptoms, it’s also listening and paying attention between the lines. When working in a second language or unfamiliar cultural context, subtle urgency cues can be missed. But they matter.

What We Can Do

Communication is not a soft skill that one is born with, it needs to be learned and a very critical skill in clinical care.

Especially for immigrant doctors, it’s the difference between being trusted or being misunderstood.

We don’t need to speak perfectly. But we do need to:

  • Ask one more question when something feels off

  • Listen to family members with equal weight as the patient

  • Clarify, repeat and confirm, not because we doubt ourselves, but because we care about being heard and showing we hear others.

This Week’s Reflection

Have you ever misread a situation because of language or cultural difference? Do you feel you’re over-explaining in fear of being misunderstood, or under-communicating to sound confident? What would help you feel more secure in high-stakes conversations?
