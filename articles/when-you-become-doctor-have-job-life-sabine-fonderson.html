When you become a doctor you have a job for life

Created on 2015-11-26 02:55

Published on 2015-11-26 05:08

When you become a doctor you have a job for life: Those were my mother's famous words when I made the decision to study medicine and become a doctor.

 My mother came from a period in history when women still had to fight and prove themselves worthy to gain acceptance in male dominated job sectors such as healthcare. In her mind, being a doctor came with prestige and lifetime security, which for a woman meant empowerment and independence.

Fast forward 20 years and I am saddened to realize that this is not the reality that once was.

According to a study performed by the BMA in 2005, one in 10 doctors could not find work. Furthermore, a third of unemployed respondents had stopped searching for NHS jobs, equating to a loss of about 900 doctors. 

In March 2014 the BMA wrote a discussion paper entitled 'Doctors at a dead end? Medical unemployment and underemployment in the UK'. The aim of the paper was to explore the likelihood of medical unemployment or underemployment in the UK and provide recommendations to reduce the risk. 

A doctor is underemployed if they are unable to find medical work during part of the normal working week or if they are unable to find sufficient medical work to yield remuneration appropriate to their experience and seniority

In an attempt to determine the cause of unemployment in doctors in the UK,  the paper states that this was largely due in part to the influx of doctors from countries like India between 2002 and 2006. Many of these doctors did not have concrete job offers and there was no coordination between the Department of Health, the Home Office and the General Medical Council.

Although registrar posts and higher speciality training post have increased since 2006, there still remains a dis-balance between the number of core training post and higher speciality training post.  This still will lead to a risk of medical unemployment within the current rigid training program. 

The paper concludes that underemployment is not necessarily catastrophic.

In light of the current opinions expressed by junior doctors with regards to their new contracts and changes in working conditions, underemployment may not necessarily be catastrophic,  I think it will be worse than that.

For instance, many doctors are considering leaving the UK to find employment in countries like Australia, New Zealand, Canada and The United States. One might even consider that this will not be such a catastrophe because the Medical Training Initiative will

Allow a small number of doctors to enter the UK from overseas for a maximum of 24 months, so that they can benefit from training and development in NHS services before returning to their home countries.

So what about the doctors that do not have the luxury to leave to exotic countries and further their careers? Many doctors will be unable to relocate due to financial responsibilities and/or  family commitments. These doctors are at risk of unemployment or underemployment. 

Nonetheless, threats to job security for doctors are a reality and are most likely to worsen in the months and years to come. 

What options do doctors have if they risk not having a job for life?
