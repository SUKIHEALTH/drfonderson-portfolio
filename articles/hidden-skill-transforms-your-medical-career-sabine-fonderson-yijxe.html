How Communication Shapes Success in Every Specialisation

If you’ve taken a career break—or switched specialisations—you probably know the anxiety of stepping into a new role. No matter how experienced you are, starting fresh in a different field can feel like stepping into a completely different world.

I know this firsthand. Over 20 years, I transitioned from clinical research → internal medicine → emergency medicine → medical insurance claims → pediatric public health → elderly care → general practice. My longest stretch was in emergency medicine, where I was deeply committed and even specialised in pediatric emergency medicine. It was my passion, my everything.

And yet, life had other plans.

Unexpected challenges forced me to pivot—not out of choice, but necessity. With no career coach to guide me (I am not even sure they existed for doctors back then), I made decisions based on survival, financial stability, and sometimes just the need to escape.

Each time, I walked into a different professional universe, where the expectations of patients, colleagues, and systems were wildly different. But what I failed to see at the time was that one thing remained constant across every single switch: communication.

When I Lost Sight of Communication

One of the most eye-opening moments in my career came when I switched from emergency medicine to insurance medicine—a job that, in hindsight, I took because of a word that starts with “b” and ends with “out.”

The role required me to assess medical fitness for work, writing reports that were scrutinized down to the last detail. The pressure was intense. Every morning, I had stomach cramps. Some days, I’d vomit before even sitting at my desk. The thought of making a wrong judgment, being called out by my supervisor, or failing to meet expectations consumed me.

One day, I assessed a young man in his 30s who claimed severe incontinence and anxiety, preventing him from working. His report read:

• Incontinence: Cannot walk more than 20m without soiling himself

• Anxiety: Needs continuous prompting to leave his house

• Social Interaction: Avoids eye contact, struggles in conversations

By all measures, he was unfit for work. But as he stood to leave, I noticed something—his rolled-up sleeve revealed a clear tan line.

“Just got back from Spain, luv,” he said casually before walking out.

My immediate reaction? Disgust. I had just been manipulated. The system was rigged.

My next patient: A truck driver with an above-knee amputation and diabetes. He was upbeat, full of life, and eager to return to work. But the system’s rules deemed him fit to work, which meant losing his benefits within four weeks.

I wanted to scream.

Had I stayed in that role longer, I might have realized something crucial: it wasn’t about expectations—it was about communication.

Communication: The Skill That Transcends Every Medical Role

What I had failed to see for years was this:

• Patients communicate strategically—sometimes consciously, sometimes unconsciously—to get what they need.

• Doctors, too, fall into communication traps—focusing on expectations, protocols, and checklists rather than truly engaging with the human in front of them.

Looking back, if I had mastered the skill of communication earlier, I would have thrived in every switch I made.

Final Thought: Mastering the Art of Career Longevity

The doctors who thrive—especially in their 40s and beyond—are not necessarily the ones with the best clinical skills. They are the ones who master communication, technology, authenticity, and delegation.

If I had understood this earlier, my transitions would have been intentional rather than reactive. But now, I use these lessons to help other doctors take control of their careers—on their terms.

Pro Tip:

Whatever the reason for your career switch or return to medicine, know this—expectations will change, but communication remains constant. Mastering how you connect with patients, clients, and colleagues is the key to shaping your ideal career and life. The better you communicate, the closer you get to a fulfilling and balanced practice.
