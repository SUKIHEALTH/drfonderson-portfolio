When You're Interrupted, Dismissed, and Still Have to Do the Right Thing

The story I share below is based on real events as I remember them, from over 15 years ago. The emotions and experiences are still vivid. Names and identifying details have been omitted to protect confidentiality and focus on the lessons learned.

I was working an evening shift in internal medicine. It wasn’t my first time caring for a patient with abdominal pain. In fact, I had worked over two years in various emergency departments in the NHS before returning to the Netherlands, where I had taken a post as a non-trainee physician.

This case was straightforward to me. A 16-year-old boy, six days of abdominal pain, nausea, and on physical examination: localized tenderness, guarding, positive psoas sign, and McBurney's point tenderness. I knew what I needed next: an ultrasound.

In my previous role as a senior locum doctor in the A&E (Accindents and Emergency), this would’ve taken minutes. But I was no longer in that role I knew. I was in a new system, in a new country, where protocol demanded I consult the on-call specialist first.

So I called.

I began:

“Hello, doctor [x], I just saw a 16-year-old boy with abdominal pains for the past six days…”

But I never got to finish.

“Why are you calling me at this time?” “I’m having dinner with my wife and kids…” “I’ll call you later.”

And then, the consultant hung up.

I waited. Then waited some more.

Afraid to call again, but more afraid to wait with a sick child in front of me, I went ahead and ordered the ultrasound.

The diagnosis? Appendicitis.

He was transferred to the surgical department and received the care he needed.

The Aftermath

The next day, I passed the same consultant in the hallway.

He stopped me, not to discuss the case or thank me, but to scold me. For acting without his permission. For doing my job.

And after his outburst, he added:

“Well, at least the kid is okay.”

Power imbalance is Real. Its even more prevalent for non-native doctors.

I had experienced power plays in the UK before. But this? This was different.

I was no longer a confident senior doctor. I was the outsider. The one who had to explain themselves twice. The one who got interrupted before finishing a sentence.

I played that conversation over in my head a hundred times. I had used the right structure. I had made the right clinical judgment. Yet none of that matters.

Later, a native-speaking colleague told me this consultant had a reputation for being difficult. She said she would flag it up. But I never heard anything back.

That experience stayed with me. In fact, it’s what pushed me to return to the UK and pursue a training in paediatric emergency medicine.

What would have helped me deal with that situation better?

A prepared response. A neutral, professional way to stand my ground. A sentence or two that respected the hierarchy but protected my role.

I wonder if a letter to the consultant a few days later would have helped?

What would you have done?

Why This Matters.

Many non-native doctors know what to do. They just don’t always have the language, permission, or protection to say it.

This edition of The Real Doctor is for every physician who has been interrupted, dismissed, or talked over and still stayed calm enough to do what was right.

You are still The Real Doctor. Let your voice and your clinical judgment be heard.
