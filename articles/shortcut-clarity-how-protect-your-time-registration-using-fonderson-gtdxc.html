What I Learned as a Claims Doctor

Before I had the job I wanted, I took the job I needed. For a while, I worked as a claims doctor. My task was to speak to people who had become ill during their holidays and wanted compensation. I’d take their story over the phone and write a structured, neutral report to support or deny the claim.

It wasn’t glamorous work. Most complaints were like:

“There were flies at the buffet.” “I ate the prawns, and the next day I was sick in bed.” “I lost two days of my vacation lying down with stomach cramps.”

It was my job to stay neutral, ask detailed questions, assess their medical history and timeline, and then write a clinical and unbiased report.

It wasn’t always easy to listen with empathy and document with precision. Most times I felt the claimants had some prepared document at hand and were reading it out over the phone. Looking back, the claims were very similarto each other except for the destinations claimants ended up being ill at. Despite my own reservations on the authenticity of some claims, doing this job taught me something I now teach others: The words we choose, especially in writing, can shape someone’s entire experience of being heard.

Months later, I met another doctor doing the same work. I asked him, “Why this job?” He said:

“I used to work as a locum doctor internal medicine. But I got tired of always being afraid of complaints. It got difficult because of the language. It felt like everything I said could be misunderstood. This job felt safer.”

His story stayed with me. Because it reminded me that so many internationally trained doctors know what they’re doing, but struggle to prove it on paper. Not because they’re not good enough. But because writing in a second language slows you down and increases your risk of being misunderstood.

Why SOEP + Snippets Matter So Much

SOEP stands for Subjective, Objective, Evaluation, Plan and is used to create a medically universal clinical structure. For immigrant doctors, it can be a lifeline. A way to bring clarity and confidence to documentation especially in any clinical situation.

But when you’re switching between languages all day long for instance, listening in Dutch, thinking in your mother tongue, and documenting back in Dutch (something I do every day) you reach a point where your brain just can’t keep up.

That’s when mistakes happen. You want to take a shortcut and omit something that was quite complex to put down in writing leads to incomplete notes. Or worse , something important gets left out and another colleague is left to figure it out.

That’s why I now use TextExpander. A quick disclaimer: I am not sponsored by TextExpander. I simply use it and recommend it because it with this tool I can:

  • Write faster and more clearly

  • Use legally sound and medically accurate phrasing

  • Avoid writing the same sentence 20 times a week

  • Reduce my cognitive load so I can focus on the patient or other administrative tasks.

Examples of snippets in Medical Claims industry:

Here are a few templates I’ve used or adapted in my own work. Whether you’re in clinical practice, writing reports, or handling telephone triage, these can save you time and protect your practice.

.travelillness

S: Patient reports gastrointestinal symptoms (diarrhea, nausea, vomiting, abdominal cramps) after consuming food at [location] during travel. O: No formal assessment abroad. Self-managed using over-the-counter meds. E: Likely foodborne gastroenteritis. P: Symptoms resolved. No further treatment required. Advised to seek timely care in future cases.

.claimsstructure

Incident Date: [insert date] Assessment Date: [insert date] Consultation Type: Telephone Summary: Patient describes onset of symptoms on [date], during stay at [location]. Reports food exposure and subsequent illness. No formal review. Symptoms lasted [X] days. Opinion: Clinical picture suggests self-limiting GI illness. Full recovery reported.

.claimimpact

Due to illness, patient was unable to attend planned excursions or enjoy key parts of the holiday. No formal leave required, but rest was necessary. No permanent limitations reported.
