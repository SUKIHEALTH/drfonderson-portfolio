Goodbye pills, welcome e-health

Created on 2019-07-08 09:36

Published on 2019-07-08 09:49

It is summer season again, most people sit on terraces and enjoy the sun's rays. I am standing in a room of the old American embassy building in The Hague, where I can give a presentation in a few minutes about stress and depression for undocumented immigrants. The window in the room cannot open. It is now clear why: what used to be a heavily protected fortress with high fences is now a building without clear utility and apparently without air conditioning. I pull the window slats and at that moment a handful of people come in. Everyone takes a seat and sits down. After a short introduction, I ask my first question: "What does stress mean?"

Fatma (not her real name) has been living in the Netherlands for 8 years without any legal documents. "I want to work, be useful, help people," she tells us. Every day she tries to keep herself busy with a visit to the neighbor or a walk outside if the weather permits. She has been to the doctor several times because of heart palpitations. He has been unable to find anything wrong with her and does his best to reassure her. A few months ago she went to the doctor again. "He did something else," Fatma says, "he prescribed a visit to the Keukenhof." She became unwell during her visit to the flower park. Back at the doctor, she was told that she has an anxiety disorder. Fatma is now waiting for an appointment with the psychologist. "That could take another 8 weeks", she was told.

Beka (not his real name) left his family in Kenya 4 years ago. Since then he has not been able to find work or send money to his family. He has a headache, and has been to the doctor twice before. Last month, Beka was prescribed medication against depression. "As long as I'm not crazy, I'm not going to swallow them," says Beka. He feels humiliated and spends most of his days alone in his room.

The stories are recognizable. Many people, regardless of their social background, experience what Fatma and Beka describe every day. If, according to the guidelines, the doctor fails, the patient will be placed on a waiting list. I recently read an article about the health benefits of e-health in people with anxiety disorders and depression. In one study, caregivers could offer patients e-mails, video consultation, chat, or telephone consultation. Fewer antidepressants were used in the group of people who were prescribed e-health compared to the group that received standard care (antidepressants, waiting or referral to a psychologist)

The power of e-health has already been proven. For example, the health gain in teledermatology is significant. With the right combination of doctor, mobile and app, patients don't have to wait for an appointment with a psychologist. Early intervention through e-health in people with (mild) psychological complaints is very effective. And it saves a lot of pills!
