Digital transformation in healthcare: Trending diabetes apps

Dr. Sabine Fonderson

Physician · Health AI Innovator · Keynote Speaker

Created on 2019-02-10 22:14

Published on 2019-02-10 22:34

In November of 1975, the founder of Sphere corporation announced the Sphere 1, the earliest micro-computers. Customers could purchase the computer as a kit to assemble themselves or one that was ready to go. Starting at $860, the Sphere 1 promised to handle “problem solving”, “record keeping needs” and one could easily “start programming”. This personal computer came complete with keyboard, a monitor and external storage.

The founder of Sphere 1 was called Michael Wise (1949-2002), he was an inventor and what we would call a ‘hacker’ of his days. He created the first known personal computer at the age of 26. Eager to let the world know about his device, he launched Sphere 1 without fully debugging it. He hoped the orders coming in would be enough to sustain his business and finance his costs. Not only did he underestimate the demand, he also delivered a product that was not flawless. In 1977, he filed for bankruptcy. What happened next was quite predictable, a huge demand to fill for personal computers was evident. It was not long before other competitors entered the playing field and improved on the work that he had already started.

Unable to make his company successful, Wise continued his work with computers and became involved in works including Macintosch. Wise also became attributed to the first microcomputer WYSIWYG word processor. He even went on to create solutions for accounting firms and owned an internet business.

Just as computers have come a long way, so has digital advances in medicine. Let us take for example the case of diabetes apps. Early programming systems could only log data by hand. There were no features to share the data between patients and doctors let alone get some sort of feedback on how to manage the data.

Medical apps should be sophisticated enough to make the lives of those using it easy. Improving on existing apps or devices should ensure that unnecessary steps are removed before getting to the end goals. Hence, they should create less work for both patients and their clinicians whilst simultaneously providing insight into their healthcare management. Two recent apps I came across that tick these requirements in diabetes management are Glooko and Tidepool.

Glooko was launched in 2011 and the app eliminates the need for patients to write down their glucose levels. The company recently partnered with Diasend ®, so that patients can upload and sync data from various glucose device meters and insulin pumps. The data is consolidated and simplified for patients and doctors. It is intuitive, fast and simple to use.

Tidepool is a not-for-profit organisation with an open-source platform to manage data from all diabetes devices. A big undertaking by the companies' owners whose children suffer from diabetes, their goal is to empower patients regarding their diabetes data. Regardless of the device or operating system, patients can sync their data, read them on a graph and share that with their doctors, friends, families and even donate their data for research purposes.

I wonder if Michael Wise, the founder of the first personal computer would be with us today had he taken advantage of the digital transformation in healthcare. In December 2002, he died from complications of diabetes. He was 53 years, 6 months and 6 days to be exact.
