Is this the season to end the A&E crisis?

Dr. Sabine Fonderson

Physician · Health AI Innovator · Keynote Speaker

Created on 2015-12-03 06:42

Published on 2015-12-03 08:22

If you live in the UK, at this time you probably know what a winter A&E crisis may resemble. Just at the start of the December month, a mass hysteria occurs in the media about how the winter crisis will affect the A&E departments the nationwide. There are talks about not meeting the '4-hour waiting times' and things like 'long bed waits'. Even worse the media will publish statements such as 'increased mortality rates in A&E during winters'.

This happens year in year out so what possible solutions are there to fix this crisis?

I came across an article published in the Nursing Standard in March 2014 entitled 'Action stations to relieve A&E: local and national efforts to avert a winter crisis in A&E were intense, but will they be enough?' by freelance journalist Alison Moore. 

Working in A&E remains demanding and stressful, and performance could still be thrown off course by bad weather

The main issues concerning the department especially during the winter are broken down into

  • An influx of elderly sick patients, the vast majority have no capacity to return to their home
  • Long waits for patients to be transferred from the emergency department to their allocated ward due to the 'no beds' syndrome. 
  • Not enough internal staff to cover the shifts and hence relying on agency staff which in turn increases expenditure. The funds could have been allocated to improving community support and services for example.

The factors that cause a winter crisis in A&E are many. I have spoken to doctors, nurses, porters, cleaners and secretaries and the despair is obvious. A&E winter crisis is always the same, everyone complains, but nothing gets done is the general consensus amongst the ones I have spoken to.

Except for the student nurse or medical student who just started their shift in A&E. They have no idea how their life is likely to change 3 months later whilst working under the most intense pressure any department has ever faced.  

Kevin Reynard and colleague wrote an article published in the British Journal of hospital medicine in 2014 entitled 'A clinical analysis of the emergency medicine workforce crisis'.  Crisis can build up in the workforce due to the signs and symptoms. For example, 

  • Symptoms of low job satisfaction can cause decreased trend in job performance  
  • Symptoms of unhappiness can cause a doctor to think of moving elsewhere to another country where the life-work blend is better. There are more A&E consultants emigrating to countries like Australia and New Zealand where the working conditions are better in terms of less pressure, better life-work blend and climate. 
  • Symptoms of stress or burnout can lead to increased vacancy rates which in turn get filled by agency work. As is recently been the case the UK government wants to impose pay caps on locum agency rates in order to save on agency staff spending. 

No matter how one looks at these factors, there will always be one constant variable, the influx of patients will continue to increase. More people live longer and have more co-morbidities which need to be cared to. Advances in technology has given people the ability to live longer but it is shocking that there is no way to create more space, improve services and recruit more professionals to care for them?

In the last year the 1.7% rise in attendances in England translates to 250 000 patients, or the equivalent of four new medium-sized emergency departments.

Solutions to the crisis are far and wide. Solutions range from

  • Create a work atmosphere that values its workforce and puts training of skills and professional development central
  • Support and encourage doctors to have a better work-life blend 
  • Step away from focusing purely on  mistakes and instead promote an environment where success is shared and encouraged
  • Recruiting more (overseas) trainees, physician associates and paramedics

Even if these departments are well staffed as I have witnessed in many A&E's as a locum the past year, there still remains one fundamental problem. How to move or transfer the patient from the department to their final destination. This is the number one thing that occupies the mind of doctors, nurses and managers. This constant pressure is no grounds for developing morale, confidence or even training skills necessary to progress in a profession.  

Maybe a solution to reduce the pathological crisis that happens in A&E is for example to make sure that

  • Patients are made aware that the A&E is not just a pit-stop to get things 'checked out' and in effect is reserved for those with real accidents or emergencies
  • Patients who do not wish to adhere to the principles of A&E are asked to pay a fee for services that they would have been able to acquire had they seen their GP
  • The waiting time of 4 hours remains the same as this is a good incentive to work effectively in the department, yet staff should be allowed the opportunity to improve their skills and not just work for the sake of working and seeing patients who in fact should not even be there. 

It is time that the symptoms and signs that are already red flags amongst healthcare providers in the A&E be taken seriously otherwise the system will collapse and not be able to withstand a crisis regardless of the season.
