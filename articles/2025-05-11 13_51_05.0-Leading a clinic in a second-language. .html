Leading a clinic in a second-language.

Created on 2025-05-11 13:51

Published on ---

Can I be honest?

Running a clinic in a second-language did not feel like walking into a room with a well-written script that can be read. In my experience, it felt like I was handed the wrong role in a play that was already done. The rest of the cast had rehearsed, were ready, had bonded and did not take kindly to any rewrites.

Even more challenging, was taking over a clinic that had history. The team had their routine down, the patients missed their adored doctor of more than 30 years. And there I was, holding the title of a practice owner. A practice that worked so well for 3 decades. Now I come in, with my second-language barrier, tyring to change a system that for the most part, any small change felt like a betrayal.

That was the heart of a conversation I had with a mentor, someone who helped me navigate this transition.

I told him:

"I know it's a process. I know I have to be patient. But in the meantime, what do I do with this frustration I feel? Because I can't keep absorbing it silently"

He replied:

"You're allowed to feel those frustrations. But the culture you want with your team has nothing to do with your language barrier. It is about building trust. Separate the people from their behaviour. And remember, frustration doesn't mean aggression. Not in leadership.

He was spot on.

I did get frustrated and it showed. I would come in early to the clinic, double-check everything, over-document just to feel safe. And sometimes I was met with silence and it made me suspicious.

It made me wonder:

"Do they even see me as a leader?"

So here's what I learned in that season and wish that someone had told me earlier.

Did my new team see me as a leader? Questioning myself like that hurt. So I knew I had to stop the self-punishment and separate the person from the behaviour and here are 5 things I learned in that process

  1. There is no need to overperform

  2. Find a person in your team to be your filter

  3. Don't take pushback personally

  4. Don't be a hostage to your own clinic

  5. Lay foundations no-one sees (yet)

As I navigated my new role as a leader, I felt the need to shrink to make others feel safe. But that behaviour is counterintuitive for any leadership role. As a leader, I have to raise the standard, and that comes with tension and diaspproval from others. Not everyone will clap and that's okay. Instead of overperforming I said:

"We're not here to do things the same way as before. If we were, this leadership change wouldn't have happened"

It was in the early stages of being a practice leader that I realised I couldn't have strategic conversations with everyone. My mentor told me to get a practice manager. Someone with whom I could align my thoughts and ambitions with. Then the practice manager would function as my "filter" and when we got together for team meetings to discuss changes, I did not feel like I was alone. We became a united front. This advice changed everything.

When the changes to come were laid out during meetings, I expected pushback and they came. The need to resist change did not mean the team was doing it maliciously. I realised it came from fear. Fear of change, fear of losing relevance. So instead of feeling frustrated and telling myself "why won't they listen?" I would explain the reason behind the changes, for instance when it came to improving our computer system security I said:

"We're changing the login system because we're legally required to. If we don't and there's a data breach, we risk losing the clinic"

As the team settled into the new system, in most part their roles did not change, however, some workflows did. In the meantime I continued to network and look at strategies to improve the clinic and the care we provided to our patients. A plan B does not mean it is bad, it means just that, in case resistance goes beyond the initial intentions, one needs to be prepared.

Which brings me to my last learning point. I learned that laying foudations that others would not see in the start was healthy and meant I spent a good amount of itme reflecting on the changes and growing my vision. The hard, slow, trust-building foundation which in truth would not win applause from team but it did protect the clinic if and when a storm should come.

Honestly, I struggled hard for a long time. It takes time and it has been worth it. Now I can look at the clinic, the team and culture we have created for ourselves that work.
